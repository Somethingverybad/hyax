How secret chats work

Messages are encrypted on your phone and decrypted only on your contact's phone. The key never travels over the network at all — each side computes it on its own. The easiest way to show how that's possible is with paint.

With paint

  1. Alice and Bob take the same shared paint, say yellow. Everyone knows it, including the eavesdropper.
  2. Each adds their own secret colour: Alice red, Bob blue. The secret never leaves them.
  3. They swap the mixtures. The eavesdropper sees an orange can and a green one.
  4. Each mixes their own secret into the other's mixture once more. Both end up with the very same brown — and that is the key.

Mixing paint is easy; un-mixing it back into colours is impossible. So the eavesdropper has yellow, orange and green, but never gets brown: that needs someone's secret.

With numbers

Instead of paint: raising to a power with a remainder. Shared numbers: p = 23 and g = 5.

  1. Alice's secret a = 6. She sends 56 mod 23 = 8.
  2. Bob's secret b = 15. He sends 515 mod 23 = 19.
  3. Alice computes 196 mod 23 = 2.
  4. Bob computes 815 mod 23 = 2.

The shared key 2 is known to both, while only 8 and 19 travelled over the network. With 23 you could brute-force the secret in a minute, but WYX uses 256-bit numbers (the P-256 elliptic curve), and brute force would take longer than the Universe has existed.

What the server sees

On both devices an encryption key is derived from the shared key (HKDF), and every message is sealed with AES-256-GCM. Only ciphertext reaches the server: bytes without a key. The server stores and delivers it but cannot read it — it never had the key.

Secret chat messages never go into the cloud cache or backups. Lose the device and the conversation is gone with it — that's the price of it existing nowhere else.

Encryption protects the channel, not the device itself. If your phone has software reading the screen or keyboard, it sees the text before encryption. Guard your phone and don't install just anything.
